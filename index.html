The Problem

Financial returns have fatter tails than statisticians would like.

Value at Risk answers a specific question: at a given confidence level, what's the worst loss we should expect over one day? Regulators and risk desks depend on the answer, so the distribution used to compute it matters enormously.

Classical models assume returns are Normally distributed, leaning on the Central Limit Theorem. Real markets disagree — crashes cluster and extreme moves happen far more often than a Gaussian curve predicts, a property called leptokurtosis. This project fits both a Normal and a Student's t distribution to ten years of the S&P 500 (ticker ^GSPC, 2,513 trading days from 2016‑01‑01 to 2026‑01‑01) and asks a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test which one the data actually supports.

Value at Risk, by definition
VaRc = −FX−1(1−c)

The c‑confidence VaR is the negated (1−c)‑quantile of the return distribution — invert the CDF, read off the loss.

Glivenko–Cantelli
supx |Fn(x) − F(x)| → 0 a.s.

The empirical CDF converges uniformly to the true CDF as sample size grows — the theoretical license to trust historical simulation.

Results

Three ways to measure the same tail risk.

Daily loss thresholds at 95% and 99% confidence, computed three ways: directly from history, from a fitted Normal, and from a fitted Student's t (ν ≈ 2.54 — very heavy tails).

Student's t fit
Dn = 0.02462  ·  p = 0.0935
Fails to reject at α = 0.05 — statistically compatible.

At 99% confidence, a desk sizing capital off the Normal model would breach its own risk limit far more often than the 1% it's budgeting for. The Student's t fit — with 2.54 degrees of freedom — tracks the historical tail closely enough to pass the goodness‑of‑fit test the Normal model fails.
